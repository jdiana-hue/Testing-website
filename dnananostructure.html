Redesigning a DNA Nanostructure for Tumor Drug Delivery

Introduction. Chemotherapy damages healthy tissue because the drug travels everywhere the bloodstream goes. One answer encases the drug in a DNA nanostructure carrying an aptamer, a short DNA sequence that recognizes a protein found on tumor tissue, so that the carrier binds to the tumor and releases its cargo there. A published tetrahedral design does this using a MUC1 aptamer for targeting and nanoparticle engines that generate oxygen to propel the structure through tissue. I'm modifying that design so it carries more drug per structure and reports on its own operation: a bigger frame for more cargo, a fourth nanoparticle engine, and a biosensor that confirms the engines are making oxygen.

Methods. I began by reverse-engineering the published structure, rebuilding it in scadnano and oxView so that it could be simulated in oxDNA. That gives me a validated baseline to measure every modification against, instead of judging a new design on its own. The current version changes the base of the frame from a triangle to a square, which creates the fourth vertex the additional engine needs.

Results, in progress. The modified structure will be assessed on whether it holds its shape. Hydrogen bond occupancy, root-mean-square fluctuation, and centroid configuration together show whether the enlarged square frame stays as stable as the triangular original. A separate calculation will confirm that four engines generate enough propulsion to move the heavier structure, since extra cargo is no use if the carrier can't reach the tumor.

Discussion. Every improvement here is also a risk to something that already works, which is why I rebuilt the baseline first, since a bigger frame may be less rigid and more cargo is more weight to push. So each change gets measured against the validated original rather than judged on its own, which is the same compare-to-a-reference approach I use in benchmarking and on the plant floor.
