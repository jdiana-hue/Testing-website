Antimicrobial Surface Testing Chamber: Senior Design

Introduction.Operating rooms depend on antimicrobial surfaces to limit the bacteria that reach a patient, but a surface only protects that patient if its performance holds under realistic exposure. Standard bench tests don't reproduce the conditions of a sterile environment, which leaves a gap between what a material claims in the laboratory and how it behaves where it matters. Our senior design team built a contained chamber that dispenses a controlled, repeatable dose of a chosen bacterium onto a candidate surface, so that growth rate and growth pattern can be measured under conditions that approximate an operating room.

Methods.The project was sponsored by a professor at Stevens Institute of Technology and carried out by a four-person team. I owned the controls subsystem, which is the part that determines whether a controlled dose is actually controlled: the electrical hardware and the software driving the dispensing mechanism. I programmed the dispense sequence on an Arduino platform in C++ and soldered the control electronics in the campus MakerSpace. I also added auxiliary features that made the chamber usable rather than just functional, the most useful being a focused light that lets an operator see the dispense zone clearly during a run.

Results.The chamber was validated outside our own department. Researchers at Binghamton University used it to test a new antimicrobial material they were developing, and Stevens students ran several different bacteria through it. Both exercises produced usable results on materials and organisms the chamber had not been designed around. The team received the Special Chemical Engineering award for the work.

Discussion.The design worked, and the best proof is that other researchers chose to run their own experiments in it, which is stronger evidence than any demonstration we could have put together ourselves because it takes our own assumptions about how the chamber would be used out of the picture. Given more time, I would instrument the dispensing mechanism to log each dose, so that every run carries its own record instead of relying on the operator. A system is only as reliable as the evidence that it runs the same way every time. That's the habit I took into every validation project since.
